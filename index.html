What we usually find

You've met this org before

Four patterns account for most of it. None is an emergency, which is exactly why they survive so long.

Someone built it in 2019. Then someone else built on top of that. Nothing here is broken enough to be an emergency — it's just heavier than it should be, every single week.

Surfaces every release

Automation that has grown teeth

Routine changes get avoided because nobody's certain what fires next. Eleven decision nodes, no descriptions, and one of them was definitely a Friday afternoon. The safest answer becomes "don't touch it" — and that answer compounds.

Surfaces every quarter close

Numbers people work around

A dashboard can run perfectly and still fail the business. When people build their own spreadsheets rather than open the report, the report isn't the problem. What's underneath it is: how the data arrives, and what happens to it on the way.

Surfaces at every process change

A process that moved on without telling Salesforce

The business changed. The org kept the receipts. Fields outlive their purpose, steps get added without old ones coming out, and eventually you're supporting three versions of the same process at once.

Surfaces every week

Work with no owner

Some problems stay open because they're hard. Others stay open because nobody turned them into a decision. There's a backlog, competing requests, and four people who can describe the issue perfectly — and no one whose job it is to close it.

The first piece of work is a document

Before anything gets changed there's a diagnostic: a fixed piece of work that ends in a written recommendation.

It starts with a conversation and a look at what you already have — how the process is meant to run, what the system is actually doing, and where the two came apart. A read-only look at the org fills in the rest.

It commits you to nothing. If it's worth acting on and you want it done, that's a separate conversation. If it isn't, you still have the document.

What arrives at the end

  • What's actually wrong, ordered by what it's costing you
  • What to do first, and why that one
  • What to leave alone
  • Where a fix is straightforward enough to do yourself

How this is being built

SFDC24 is a small team of AI agents working on a shared board, with a human making every consequential decision. This page, the assessment model behind it and the tooling underneath were all built that way, and we would rather show the method than describe it.

The agents review each other, and reject each other's work. Every change is reviewed at an exact commit by an agent that did not write it, and a reviewer is expected to reproduce a claim rather than accept it. In one recent night, eight real defects were found in work that had already been called finished. Six were found in the first agent's own work, by a second one running a check the author had not.

That is the whole argument. Not that an AI wrote the code — anyone can say that. That being wrong here is cheap, gets caught in minutes, and the reasoning is written down where it can be read afterwards. A single assistant cannot do that, because the check it would naturally run is the one blind to its own gap.

Where this actually is. Research stage. The assessment model is real and scores a live org against four measures. The connector that reads a client org is being built now. We are not going to describe the internals here, and we are not going to claim a product that is still becoming one.
