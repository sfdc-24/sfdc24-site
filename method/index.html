SFDC Method

What we built, and what it means for your Salesforce work

Over ten weeks, a small team of AI agents built this site and now runs it in the open, with a human making the decisions that matter. This page sets out what was built, what that delivery model means for a Salesforce engagement, and where the work honestly stands. The offer is assessment, automation and AI enablement — research stage, and we would rather say so than pretend otherwise.

How a piece of work moves

One request becomes several parallel workstreams, each change is checked by someone who did not make it, and nothing goes live until its required automated checks pass.

What it means for your Salesforce work

Parallel workstreams

Several agents work at the same time on separate branches: one analyses, one builds, one tests. Work that would queue behind a single resource runs side by side, so the elapsed time to a reviewed change shrinks. In September alone, 206 pull requests were merged.

Independent review before anything ships

Every change is checked by an agent that did not write it, and approved by a person before release. Where agents produce competing versions, Jev, the team’s gatekeeper, a fast, low-cost decision model, scores and ranks them, so a heavyweight model is never the judge. The owner still makes the call.

Guarded releases

A change reaches the live site only through a pull request, and only once its required automated checks pass on the up-to-date commit; the main branch refuses anything else. That sits on top of the independent review and the person who approves. A preview build before release is available, and optional. The release record, by commit, is on Releases.

Salesforce focus, starting with a diagnostic

The work is led by a certified Salesforce Sales Cloud Consultant and stays on the Salesforce platform: assessment, automation and AI enablement. It starts with a fixed diagnostic that ends in a written recommendation, so decisions are made on evidence before anything in your org is changed.

What we usually find

Four patterns account for most of it. None is an emergency, which is exactly why they survive so long.

Surfaces every release

Automation that has grown teeth

Eleven decision nodes, no descriptions. Nobody is certain what fires next, so the safest answer becomes "don't touch it" — and that compounds.

Surfaces every quarter close

Numbers people work around

A dashboard can run perfectly and still fail the business. When people build their own spreadsheets rather than open the report, the report isn't the problem — how the data arrives is.

Surfaces at every process change

A process that moved on without telling the system

The business changed. The org kept the receipts. Fields outlive their purpose, and you end up supporting three versions of one process at once.

Surfaces every week

Work with no owner

Some problems stay open because nobody turned them into a decision. Four people can describe the issue perfectly; none of them owns closing it.

The first piece of work is a document

Before anything gets changed there's a diagnostic: a fixed piece of work that ends in a written recommendation. It starts with a conversation and a look at what you already have. A read-only look at the org fills in the rest.

It commits you to nothing. If it isn't worth acting on, you still have the document.

What arrives at the end

  • What's actually wrong, ordered by what it's costing you
  • What to do first, and why that one
  • What to leave alone
  • Where a fix is straightforward enough to do yourself

How this is built, and where it actually is

This board is a small team of AI agents working on a shared board, with a human making every consequential decision. The agents review each other, and reject each other's work. Every change is reviewed at an exact commit by an agent that did not write it, and a reviewer is expected to reproduce a claim rather than accept it — which is how eight real defects were caught in one night, in work already called finished.

Where this actually is. Research stage, and here is the honest boundary. The scoring model is real: four pillars, defects against a defined opportunity set, a sigma level and a grade. What does not exist yet is the part that reads a live org. Nothing here has scored anyone's live system yet. The console that shows a readout runs on synthetic data and says so on its own page, in four places, because a demo that quietly passes for a customer result is the one thing that would make the rest of this worthless. The connector is designed and not built. We are not going to describe the internals here, and we are not going to claim a product that is still becoming one.

How the board fits

The communication board on the sheet is the system of record. A coordination layer is being built beside it. That layer is not live on this site, and it does not replace the board. This page does not publish a second status panel for it.

Measured figures stay on the Ops page (scorecard, Gantt, CI, lane rates) and in its delivery snapshot (in progress and landed). Those reads are the published snapshot. They are not a live coordination bus. The comparison between the sheet and the coordination layer is not green, so no coordination-layer count is shown.
